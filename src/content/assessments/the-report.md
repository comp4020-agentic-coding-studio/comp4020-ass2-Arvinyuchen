Two thousand words, plus your record. You run the experiment you pre-registered
in week 6 and you report what happened.

## The largest criterion is inference, not result

Forty-five per cent of this piece is whether your conclusion follows from your
data. Not whether your day improved. Not whether the intervention worked. Not
whether you found anything at all.

A report that says *"I measured this carefully for two weeks, and the honest
answer is that I cannot tell, and here is precisely why"* is a strong report. It
may be the strongest kind. A report that says *"this worked, I feel much
better"*, on a design that cannot support the claim, is a weak one no matter how
true the feeling is.

This is not a stylistic preference. **Marking the outcome would reward regression
to the mean**: most of you began your experiments on an unremarkable-to-bad
stretch, and most of you will have improved regardless of what you did. A course
that handed out marks for improvement would be handing them out for arithmetic,
and week 9 exists to show you exactly that.

## Declare every departure

You will have deviated from your pre-registration. Everybody does: a measure
turns out to take too long, a week gets eaten, the stopping rule fires.

Deviating is not penalised. **Failing to declare it is**, and it is the one thing
in this course that reads as dishonest rather than inexperienced. List every
change, when you made it, and whether you had seen any data at the time.

## Null results

If nothing happened, report that nothing happened, at the same length and with
the same care you would have given a positive result.

The literature has spent decades learning this the hard way (week 12 covers why
journals now insist on registration) and the personal version is the same. A
semester of reports that all found something would tell us only that we had
taught you to find things.
