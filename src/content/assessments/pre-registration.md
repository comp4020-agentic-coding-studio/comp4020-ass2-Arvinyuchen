Two pages, handed in before you start. That ordering is the entire point, and it
is the only deadline in this course that cannot be extended after the fact: a
[pre-registration](/glossary/#pre-registration) submitted once you have seen your
data is not a pre-registration, whatever it says on it.

What you are planning is one change to one block of your own day, and the
authority for the change is public guidance rather than your own preference.
Nothing here is run this week. You run it from here to week 10 and report it
then, and in week 12 you run part of it again and account for the difference.

## What goes in it

1. **The block.** One block from your week 1 day, taken from the ones you marked
   as a block you would be willing to run differently and could actually reach.
   That mark was a note to yourself for later. This is later. If you kept the day
   on paper, or marked nothing, choose the block now and say that you chose it
   now.
2. **The guideline.** The published guidance that covers that block, named as a
   document rather than as a thing everybody knows. Week 1's lecture is where to
   start looking, and the map at the foot of it is there so you can see what
   shapes these documents come in before you go hunting for one.
3. **The four questions**, from week 2's lecture, in the order it asks them. Is
   it still in force. Who is it written for. Is it a recommendation or a tip. How
   sure is it. That order is not decoration: they are ordered by how much each
   one can save you, cheapest first, so that the expensive question is only ever
   asked of a sentence that has already survived the cheap ones. Where the
   document does not answer, write down that it does not answer. "This page
   carries no certainty rating and holds the evidence in a separate report" is a
   finding, and it is marked as one.
4. **The change.** One sentence for what you will do differently in that block,
   and one for which part of the guidance it comes from. Where the guidance stops
   short of the thing you are changing, say where it stops and that the rest is
   yours. Guidance narrows to a group and then runs out, and every reader of it
   has to close that last gap themselves.
5. **The measure**, [operationalised](/glossary/#operationalise) as in week 2's
   lab: what you will write down, when, and how long one recording takes. Then
   the part week 3 was for. State the smallest difference this measure could
   detect, and where that figure came from. Your two instruments disagreed by
   some amount on one night in week 3, and a difference smaller than that
   disagreement is one you have already shown yourself you cannot see. If the
   measure is new this week, say how you will find its spread before you rely on
   it.
6. **The stopping rule.** When you stop, and what would make you stop earlier
   than that. Fixed now, in writing, the way week 5 fixed the end of its removal
   phase before the removal began. Every week of this course above the lowest
   risk publishes one before it asks you to do anything, and a plan to change
   your own day owes you the same.
7. **What would prove you wrong.** A specific result, a number, a direction or a
   pattern, that you would accept as the change having failed.

## One worked trace

The block is two hours at a desk in the evening. The guidance is Australia's
24-hour movement guidelines, on the page written for adults aged 18 to 64, which
recommend limiting and breaking up sedentary time. The four questions: the page
prints the date it was last updated, so somebody has looked at it recently; the
adult page is the one addressed to a reader of that age, and there are separate
pages for readers who are not; breaking up sedentary time sits among the
recommendations and not under the heading marked Tips; and the page carries no
certainty rating and no strength rating at all, with the supporting evidence held
in a separate final report. So the sentence is in force, it is addressed to you,
it is a recommendation rather than a tip, and how sure it is, this page will not
say.

What the guidance does not tell you is how long a break is, or where in a
Thursday evening to put one. That is the part you are writing, and saying so in
the document is worth more than quietly letting the guideline appear to have
covered it.

## Why the last one carries the most marks

Because it is the one that cannot be written afterwards. Everything else in the
list can be reconstructed from memory once you know how things turned out, and
reconstructed honestly, with no intent to mislead. The block was in your week 1
record, the guideline is still published, the measure is in your hand and the
stopping rule is a decision you could describe truthfully in October. What
cannot be reconstructed is what you would have counted as failure before you
knew. Writing it down in advance is the only way that sentence means anything,
and it is why this piece is due before the fortnight rather than after it.

## A note on what is being marked

Your expectation does not have to be correct: a comprehensively wrong prediction
can score full marks. You are marked on whether the document was specific enough
to be wrong at all. Nobody reading it knows how your fortnight went, and nothing
in the mark waits to find out, because marking the outcome would hand the marks
to whoever happened to start on a bad week and return to an ordinary one. That
is arithmetic rather than inference, and week 9 is where you watch it happen to
somebody who thought it was a result.
