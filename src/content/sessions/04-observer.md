Last week the instrument disturbed your sleep. This week the instrument is you.

[Reactivity](/glossary/#reactivity) is the name for it: the act of measuring
changes the thing measured. You already met a version of this in week 1, when
recording your day made you want to improve it. Now we stop treating that as a
nuisance and start treating it as a quantity.

## The exercise

Log every time you pick up your phone, by hand, on paper, for three days.

Not the built-in screen-time figure. By hand. The awkwardness is the instrument:
writing a tally mark is slow enough that you will sometimes decide not to pick
the phone up rather than record it.

That decision is the data. You are not measuring phone use. You are measuring the
gap between your phone use and your phone use while being watched by yourself.

## Careful with the story

The effect is usually taught through the Hawthorne Works — a factory outside
Chicago where, between 1924 and 1932, researchers changed the lighting and
workers' output went up, and went up again when they changed it back, apparently
because the workers knew they were being studied.

It is a good story and the phrase "Hawthorne effect" is now permanent. It is also
shakier than it sounds. When economists went back to the surviving illumination
data decades later, the pattern that the story rests on was much fainter than the
retelling suggests.

So we are in an odd position: the effect is real and you will observe it in
yourself this week, but the famous demonstration of it is not strong evidence.
Both things are true and you should be able to hold them at once. Getting that
distinction right — between *this happens* and *this study showed it happens* —
is most of what this course is for.

## What comes next

From week 5 you start intervening. Everything you do from here on will be
contaminated by this week's finding, permanently and unavoidably, and the only
available response is to know roughly how large it is.

Write that number down. You will want it in week 10.
