Here is the most useful idea in this course, and it takes one sentence:
**unusual measurements tend to be followed by ordinary ones.**

That is [regression to the mean](/glossary/#regression-to-the-mean), and once you
have seen it you cannot unsee it in any advice you are ever given again.

## Why it matters so much here

Think about when a person starts something new. Nobody takes up a sleep routine
on a week they have been sleeping beautifully. Nobody reorganises their whole day
on a day that went well. People change things on their worst days. That is
what a bad enough day is for.

So the intervention begins at the bottom. And from the bottom, the next few days
are very likely to be better, for the same reason that an unusually cold Tuesday
is usually followed by a less cold Wednesday: the bottom is not where most days
are.

This means an intervention that does absolutely nothing will look like it worked.
Not sometimes. Reliably, and to a degree that feels convincing from the inside,
because you genuinely did feel better afterwards. You are not being fooled about
how you felt. You are being fooled about why.

## What this does to the last four weeks of your own work

In week 5 you took something away and put it back, and you probably found
something. In week 8 you started keeping track of what else was happening at the
same time. This week is the third cut: even with a clean design and an honest
record of everything else going on, if you began your experiment on an unusual
day, part of your result is the day being unusual.

Go back to your week 1 record and find your strangest day. Then look at the three
days after it. That is the size of the effect you would have claimed if you had
started an experiment right there.

## The one that stings

Galton described the pattern in 1886, in parents and their children's heights.
The version worth carrying around is Kahneman's. He was teaching flight
instructors, who had noticed that praising a cadet after an exceptional landing
seemed to make the next landing worse, while tearing strips off one after a bad
landing seemed to help. They had built a whole training philosophy on it.

An exceptional landing is followed by a more ordinary one. So is a terrible one.
The instructors had spent years drawing a lesson about praise and criticism from
a fact about arithmetic, and the lesson they drew was to be harsher with people.

## What we do in the lab

We pool the class's week 1 records, pick out every unusually bad day, and look at
what followed. The effect will be visible without any statistics at all, which is
the point: this is not a subtle artefact that only shows up under analysis. It is
the shape of the data.

Then we ask the uncomfortable question. Everyone in this room has been helped by
something. How much of it was this?
