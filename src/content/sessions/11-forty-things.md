Log twenty things about yourself for four days. Thirty is better. Sleep, mood,
steps, coffee, water, screen time, how many people you spoke to, what you ate,
the weather, whether you wore the good jacket.

Then go looking for patterns. You will find them. They will be real numbers,
correctly calculated, and mostly meaningless.

## The arithmetic of finding things

Twenty variables give you a hundred and ninety possible pairs. At four days each,
some of those pairs will line up beautifully by chance alone, not because you
made an error, but because that is what chance does at that many opportunities.

[Multiple comparisons](/glossary/#multiple-comparisons) is the name for this
problem, and its signature is a specific kind of story: *I discovered that I sleep
better on days I drink more water.* The number behind that sentence can be
impeccable. The sentence is still probably about nothing, because it was chosen
from a hundred and eighty-nine alternatives after the fact.

This is week 6's lesson arriving from a different direction. There, the danger
was deciding what you were testing after seeing the result. Here, the danger is
testing everything and reporting the winner.

## The salmon

In 2009 a group of neuroscientists put a dead Atlantic salmon into an fMRI
scanner and showed it photographs of people in social situations, asking it to
determine what emotion the people were experiencing. They then analysed the scan
the way many papers did at the time.

The salmon showed significant brain activity.

The salmon was dead and on a fishmonger's slab hours earlier. The point of the
exercise was that a brain scan contains tens of thousands of voxels, each one a
separate test, and if you do not correct for how many questions you asked, you
will find answers in a fish.

## What this does to your own dashboard

Every self-tracking application you have ever used does this by design. It shows
you the correlation it found, not the two hundred it looked at. It cannot tell you
how many comparisons went into the one insight it surfaced, and usually it does
not know.

So the assessable part of this week is not a finding. It is a number: how many
comparisons did your dataset make available? Once you can say that out loud, the
insights get much quieter, and much more trustworthy.
