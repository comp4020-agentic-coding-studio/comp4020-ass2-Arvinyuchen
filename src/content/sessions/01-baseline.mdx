## What we do in the lab

A [baseline](/glossary/#baseline) is your day as it already is, before anyone
interferes with it. Every later week in this course compares something against
this one, so if you spend week 1 tidying, you spend the remaining eleven weeks
measuring against a day that never happened. There is no hypothesis this week,
nothing to prove and nothing to beat, and the only way to build it badly is to
make it interesting.

So we record twenty-four hours at whatever resolution you can actually sustain:
fifteen minutes is plenty, and an hour is not useless. Paper is fine. Your
phone is fine. What matters is that the record is continuous and that you wrote
it during the day rather than reconstructing it that night.

Then we do the part that feels like nothing: we read our own days back to each
other and notice what we left out. Everybody leaves something out. The
interesting question is not what you did with your Tuesday, it is which parts of
Tuesday you did not think were worth recording.

There is a sheet for all of it below, if a screen suits you better than paper.
It asks when you woke up and draws a line, and you pull that line down to open
a block: how far you pull it is how long the block lasted, a minute to the
pixel, and the space it opens is where you write what you were doing. At the
end of the day you press the sleep button, which records everything from there
back to waking as sleep and closes the twenty-four hours. Nothing has to be
dragged, and for most people typing is faster: every time on the sheet is a
field, and Enter walks from one block to the next. What it does not do is ask
you what you left out: that part is the hour in the lab, not a box on a
screen. It keeps nothing. It saves no copy, sends nothing anywhere, and loses
the lot when the tab closes, so print the sheet or save it as a PDF before you
leave the page.

## The block you were not awake for

The sheet closes on the one stretch of the day you could not write down while it
happened. Every other hour goes on the page from inside it, which is the whole
instruction and the reason the sheet exists; sleep is the only part of the
twenty-four hours that has to be reconstructed afterwards, in the morning, from
a night you were absent for. You will put something in that block anyway,
because the day has to close, and what you put there is a different kind of
record from everything above it.

If you wear a watch, or your phone spent the night on the mattress, something
was awake for those hours and wrote down what it thought was going on. The
reader below opens one night of that. You give it the date of the day you are
recording and the `export.xml` file out of the Health app, and it tells you what
the instrument had you doing between midday on that day and midday on the next.
That is the whole of what it does. It does not score your night, does not tell
you what any of it means, and does not put its numbers beside yours: reading two
instruments against each other is week 3, and turning up to that week having
already done it is worse than turning up cold.

None of this stops week 1 being a baseline. You are not asked to sleep
differently, to go to bed at a different time, or to lie there knowing you are
being measured, and the night you open is one that has already finished. There
is also a route through all of it without a watch and without a file, described
on the block itself, and it is the week's own method rather than a consolation
prize.

Which leaves the question of why a lab that changes nothing carries a stopping
rule at the top of the page. It is not the day that can cost you something here,
it is the data: a number about your own body, produced overnight by a device,
is the first thing in this course with any power to bother you. Week 3 gives
that its name, its evidence and the full version of the rule. This week has the
mild form of it, and the mild form still gets a rule.
