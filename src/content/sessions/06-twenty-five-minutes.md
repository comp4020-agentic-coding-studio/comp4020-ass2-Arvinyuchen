The technique is named after a tomato. Francesco Cirillo, a university student in
the late 1980s who could not concentrate, picked up a tomato-shaped kitchen timer
and bet himself he could study, really study, for ten minutes. That is the origin
as his own 2006 paper tells it. The account on his site today says the question
was two minutes, which is worth knowing before you trust any other detail of the
story.

That is the entire provenance of the number. One person, one timer, and an
interval that the paper dates to 1992 without ever comparing it against another
interval. It was put online for free in 2006, and is now taught in schools, built
into hundreds of applications, and recommended to you as though it were a
property of human attention.

## The awkward part

This week's reading list has a hole in it, and the hole is the lesson.

We have no effect claim to offer you about twenty-five minutes. Not a weak one,
not a contested one: none. There is no trial establishing that twenty-five
minutes beats forty, or eighteen, or however long you happen to be able to
concentrate today. The interval is not disputed in the literature so much as
absent from it.

Cirillo, to his credit, has never claimed otherwise. His own paper describes
twenty-five as what worked for him and then recommends thirty in its results
section, which is a stronger admission than most of his readers have ever made.
The overreach happened downstream, in everyone who repeated the number without
reading the document it came from.

## So we test it

You will compare two work-block lengths. Twenty-five minutes against something
else: your own guess, forty minutes, whatever you suspect might suit you better.
One variable, two conditions.

The difficulty is order. If you run twenty-five minutes all week and forty the
week after, then "the second one was better" and "I got better at this" are the
same sentence, and you cannot tell them apart.
[Counterbalancing](/glossary/#counterbalancing) is the fix: you alternate, so
that being second happens equally often to both conditions. It is a small
administrative discipline that buys you a real comparison.

Before you start, you will write down what you expect, how you will measure it,
and what result would make you conclude the timer had lost. That document is a
[pre-registration](/glossary/#pre-registration), and handing it in before you
collect anything is the first assessed piece of work in this course. It exists
because the alternative is deciding what you were testing after you have seen how
it went, which is how you end up with a technique that works for everyone and
evidence for no one.

## What to bring

Your week 5 record, because we are reusing the discipline you built there. A
guess about your own best interval, written down, so you cannot revise it
afterwards. And a timer that is not on your phone, if you can find one, not for
any measured reason, but because Cirillo's wasn't, and it is worth noticing how
different the same instruction feels when it cannot also show you a
notification.
