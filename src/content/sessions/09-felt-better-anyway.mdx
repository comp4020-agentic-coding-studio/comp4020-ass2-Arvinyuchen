Bring your week 5 record. Today you measure the improvement you get for doing
nothing.

Unusual measurements tend to be followed by ordinary ones, so the day after your
worst day was always going to be better, whatever you did about it. That is
[regression to the mean](/glossary/#regression-to-the-mean). The lecture is where
it gets argued; this hour is where you find out how large it is in your own
numbers, which is the version you will need for the report.

## What your record has to look like

Week 5 is the only run of consecutive daily numbers you own: one measure, one
value a day, across a phase where you measured the thing as it was, a phase
where you took something away, and a phase where you put it back. Nothing else
in the course will do. Week 1 is a single day, week 3 is a single night, week 4
is three days and so has no middle worth the name, and week 6 alternates its two
conditions from one day to the next by design, so two days running there differ
by more than chance.

You need eight days or more, and you need to be able to find three pairs of
consecutive days in it that each sit inside one phase. If your record is shorter
than that, or if the gaps fall in the wrong places, take the previous year's
anonymised record instead and run the same six steps on it. That is the same
exercise and it costs you nothing.

## In the lab, in order

1. **Lay the record out.** Your numbers in date order, one row a day, every row
   marked A, B or C for its phase. Leave a gap where a day is missing rather
   than filling it in.
2. **Find your middle day.** Copy the numbers, put them in order of size, take
   the one in the middle. If you have an even number of days, take the lower of
   the two.
3. **Find your range.** Your highest day minus your lowest day. One number, and
   everything below is a fraction of it.
4. **Work out a distance.** A day's distance is how far its number sits from
   your middle day, ignoring which side it falls on, divided by your range.
   Every distance comes out between 0 and 1. Your lowest day's distance and your
   highest day's distance add to exactly 1, which is how you check the
   arithmetic before you go on.
5. **Take three pairs**, each of them two consecutive days inside a single
   phase. The low pair is your lowest day and the day straight after it. The
   high pair is your highest day and the day straight after it. The control pair
   is the earliest day whose distance is under a quarter, with the day straight
   after it, and it uses none of the four days already spoken for. If an extreme
   day is the last day of its phase, use your next lowest or next highest day
   that is not.
6. **Six distances on the board.** The board has three columns, low, high and
   control, and you add your pair to each of them. No name, no units and no
   dates go up with them.

## Reading the board

Each column holds pairs of the same shape: how far a day sat from that person's
middle, and how far the next day sat. That is Galton's two columns with days in
place of families, which is why the lab is built this way and not some other
way.

The first distance in a low pair or a high pair is large, because that is how the
day was chosen. The second one is not chosen at all, and the question the board
answers is where it lands. If it lands down among the control distances, then a
day picked for being extreme is followed by a day that is not, and an
intervention starting on the extreme day would have been credited with the whole
of that move.

If the second distances in the low and high columns are not visibly smaller than
the first, say so out loud. The published evidence is clear about which way this
goes, and one room's fortnight in March is not the published evidence. A board that
refuses to show the effect is the most interesting hour we could have, and it is
reported as a result rather than quietly re-run.

<Callout type="info">
  There is an objection to make at step 5, and it is half right. The day after
  your lowest day cannot be lower than your lowest day, so the value could only
  go up, and an exercise that put the direction on the board would be rigged by
  construction. That is exactly why the direction is not what goes up there. A
  distance from your middle day is free to be anything from 0 up to the largest
  distance in your record, so the second number in each pair can land anywhere,
  and where it actually lands is the finding. If you spot the rigged version of an
  argument before anyone tells you it is rigged, you are doing the thing this
  course is for.
</Callout>

## What the board cannot tell you

It cannot tell you how much of your own week 5 result was this. Every person on
the board is measuring something different on a scale of their own, so the
distances are comparable in shape and not in units, and no total from them means
anything. Nor can eight or ten days each settle how large the effect is in
general: the blood pressure figures below rest on five pooled studies read at
long-term follow-up, and the class board rests on one fortnight in March.

There is a second reading of the low column, and week 8 is why it has to be
named. A day is unusual because several things went wrong at once, and those
things, a deadline and a cold and an argument, mostly end. Part of what the board
shows is those things ending. That is not a rival explanation to what you are
looking at, it is the same one told from underneath, and it is the reason there
is no mechanism to go hunting for.

## What you take to the report

Two things, and both are small. The first is the day your removal phase began,
and its distance, which tells you where in your own record the experiment
started. The second is the sentence, written before you leave the room: why an
intervention begun on your lowest day would have looked successful, said without
using the words regression to the mean. If you can only say it with the phrase,
you have the label and not the idea, and the label is worth no marks at all.
