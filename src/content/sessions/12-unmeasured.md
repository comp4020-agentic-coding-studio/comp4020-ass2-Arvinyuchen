This week you do not measure anything.

That is the exercise, and for a certain kind of student it is the hardest one in
the course.

## The course cannot give you an answer

You came in wanting to know when to sleep, whether to eat breakfast, how long to
work. Twelve weeks on, the honest position is this: you have found some things out
about yourself, and every one of them has an expiry date, because week 10 showed
you that the subject keeps moving.

That is not a disappointing result. It is the result. Any course that sent you
away with a list would be selling you the same thing the advice industry sells —
a population average with your name written on it, which in this case happens to
be a sample of one, measured in March.

What survives is not the answer. It is that you now know how to ask again.

## Stopping is a skill

Everything in this course had a stopping rule, and by now you should have noticed
that the stopping rules were the most useful writing on each page. They were there
for your safety, but they were also doing methodological work: a measurement
without a planned ending quietly becomes a habit, and a habit is not an
experiment. It is just something you do now.

So: pick one measurement to keep, and say for how long. Pick one to stop today.
Both choices are assessed, and the second is worth more, because it is the one
nobody does voluntarily.

## Why registries exist

By the early 2000s medical journal editors had a problem they could no longer
ignore. Trials were reporting outcomes that had been chosen after the results came
in, and trials with unwelcome results were not being published at all — so the
literature was a record of what had worked rather than what had been tried.

The fix was procedural, not statistical: register the trial, with its outcomes, in
public, before enrolling anybody. From mid-2005 the major journals would not
publish anything that had not done so.

You did the personal version of this in week 6. It is the same instrument at a
different scale, and it exists for the same reason — not because researchers are
dishonest, but because nobody, including you, can be trusted to remember what they
expected once they know what happened.

## The last hour

We spend it on the day you did not measure. What did you notice without writing
anything down? What did you miss?

Some of you will find the unmeasured day a relief and some of you will find it
unnerving, and both reactions are worth saying out loud, because they tell you
something about what the measuring was for.
