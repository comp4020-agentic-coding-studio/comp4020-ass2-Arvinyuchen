"I'm tired" is not a measurement. It is a report, and a compressed one: it can
mean your eyes hurt, or you are bored, or you have been awake for nineteen hours,
or you do not want to do the thing you are about to do.

To [operationalise](/glossary/#operationalise) a word is to decide which of those
you mean, and to pick something you can write down. The decision looks like
bookkeeping. It is not. It settles in advance what your experiment is capable of
noticing.

## Three ways to be tired

Bring three candidates to the lab. You will find they disagree, which is the
useful part.

- **Ask yourself.** A number from one to seven, recorded four times a day. Fast,
  free, and the thing almost every study relies on.
- **Time something.** How long it takes you to react to a light, or to read a
  paragraph, or to fall asleep. Slower to collect, harder to argue with.
- **Count a consequence.** Typing errors. Times you reread a sentence. Times you
  walked into a room and forgot why.

## Why this is the week it matters

There is a well-known sleep restriction study in which people spent two weeks on
four, six or eight hours in bed. Their own sleepiness ratings rose sharply for
a few days and then flattened out. After a while, four hours and six hours
felt about the same. Their measured performance did not flatten out. It kept
declining, steadily, the whole time.

The people in that study were not lying and they were not careless. They had simply
lost the ability to feel the thing that was happening to them. If the only
measure you carry is "ask yourself", there is a version of tiredness you are
structurally unable to detect.

Your week 1 record already has some version of this in it. Most people wrote
something like "flat" or "fine" at some point on that day. This week you decide
what you would have to have written down instead.

## The trap

You will be tempted to pick the measure that is easiest to collect and then
quietly treat it as though it measured everything. Everybody does this. The
defence is to write down, now, one thing your chosen measure cannot see, and to
keep that sentence next to your results for the rest of the semester.
