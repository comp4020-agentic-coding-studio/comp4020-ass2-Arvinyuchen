## The shape before the name

An unusual measurement is usually followed by an ordinary one. That sentence is
the whole of this week, and it is not a claim about your body, your willpower or
your routine: it is a property of any two measurements that are related without
being identical. Nothing acts on the second reading. There is no rebound, no
correction and no debt being repaid. An extreme reading is extreme because
several things went wrong at once, and several things rarely go wrong at once
twice in a row, so the next reading sits closer to where most of your readings
already are. Now set that beside the question of when people actually change
something. Nobody takes up a sleep routine in a week they have been sleeping
beautifully. Nobody rebuilds a morning on a morning that went fine. Changes
start at the bottom, which means the days after a change begins are very likely
to be better than the days before it, whatever the change was, and including no
change at all. That is an improvement available for free to anything whatsoever,
and it has been sold a great many times. It has a name,
[regression to the mean](/glossary/#regression-to-the-mean), and the rest of
this lecture is three occasions on which it was mistaken for a result.

## Three times this has already fooled somebody

### Galton measured it and filed it under heredity

Francis Galton read [Regression towards Mediocrity in Hereditary
Stature](https://galton.org/essays/1880-1889/galton-1886-jaigi-regression-stature.pdf)
to the Anthropological Institute and published it in 1886, and it is the first
place anyone put numbers to this. He had collected family records by offering
prizes for them, and Table I of that memoir cross tabulates the heights of 205
mid-parents against the heights of their 928 adult children, where a mid-parent
is the average of a couple with the mother's height first multiplied by 1.08 to
put it on the same scale as the father's. What matters is the last column, which
gives the median height of the children born to each height of mid-parent, set
against the mid-parent height itself.

<GaltonMedians />

Read the two columns against each other and the whole table leans inward. The
71.5 inch mid-parents had children with a median height of 69.9, more than an
inch and a half shorter than their parents, while the 64.5 inch mid-parents had
children with a median of 65.8, better than an inch taller than their parents,
and every row in between moves the same way by a smaller amount the closer it
starts to the middle. Galton's own summary is that the height deviate of the
offspring is on average two thirds of the height deviate of its mid-parentage.
One row refuses: the 72.5 inch mid-parents have a median child height of 72.2,
which barely moves at all, and that row rests on 19 children of 6 mid-parents,
which is its own lesson about reading a single line of any table. What Galton
did next is the error the rest of this lecture is about. He treated the pattern
as a fact about inheritance, a tendency of a race to fall back towards its type,
and it is nothing of the kind.

<Callout type="info">
  Regression to the mean is not a force, and the commonest way to misunderstand
  it is to look for the mechanism. There isn't one. Galton's own title, with its
  regression towards mediocrity, reads as though heights were being pulled
  somewhere by something, and they are not: the same arithmetic turns up in
  blood pressure, in exam marks and in aircraft landings, anywhere two
  measurements of the same thing agree with each other without agreeing
  exactly. If you catch yourself asking why the days after a bad day improve,
  you have already accepted a cause that does not exist.
</Callout>

### The flight instructors built a training philosophy on it

The sharpest case is in Tversky and Kahneman's ["Judgment under Uncertainty:
Heuristics and
Biases"](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~bskyrms/bio/readings/tversky_k_heuristics_biases.pdf),
published in *Science* in 1974, volume 185, pages 1124 to 1131, and it is worth
reading in their words rather than in the retellings that came later. "In a discussion of flight
training, experienced instructors noted that praise for an exceptionally smooth
landing is typically followed by a poorer landing on the next try, while harsh
criticism after a rough landing is usually followed by an improvement on the
next try. The instructors concluded that verbal rewards are detrimental to
learning, while verbal punishments are beneficial, contrary to accepted
psychological doctrine." The instructors were reporting what they saw
accurately. An exceptional landing is followed by a more ordinary one, and so is
a terrible one, whether or not anybody says a word in between. The authors then
generalise it past flying: "the human condition is such that, by chance alone,
one is most often rewarded for punishing others and most often punished for
rewarding them". Years of experience, an honest record of it, and a conclusion
about how to treat people that came out of arithmetic rather than out of the
cadets.

### The self-improvement industry gets this for free

The instructors at least had to wait for a bad landing. Anyone selling a routine
does not, because their customers arrive already at the bottom: people go
looking for a new system on the week the old one visibly failed, which is the
week their measurements are furthest from their own average. Whatever they buy
is therefore measured from the worst point, against the days that follow it, and
those days were going to be better anyway. This is not an argument that such
products do nothing, and it is not an invitation to be cynical about people who
say they were helped. It is an argument about one particular kind of evidence.
The testimonial that opens "I was at my lowest and then I tried this" is the
shape of the data being reported as the effect of the product, and no amount of
sincerity in the telling can separate the two. Only a comparison can, which
means something else that also started at the bottom.

You have all of this in your own records, which is where today's lab starts: we
pool the class's week 5 records, the one run of consecutive daily numbers any of
you owns, and set how far each unusually bad day sat from its own record's middle
against how far the day after it sat. The week 1
[baseline](/glossary/#baseline) is a single day, so it cannot show you what
follows anything. No statistics are involved, because none are needed to see it. Then take it to your report, due next week, and to the
paragraph in it that will be hardest to write. Find the day your experiment
began and say where that day sat in your own distribution. If it was
unremarkable, say so and move on. If it was one of your worst, then some part of
your result belongs to the day rather than to what you changed, and the honest
version says how much of it you cannot separate out. The report is marked on
inference rather than on outcome for precisely this reason: marks for
improvement would be marks for arithmetic, and this is the week that shows you
why.
