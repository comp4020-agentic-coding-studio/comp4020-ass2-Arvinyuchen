## What a guideline is made of

You look up the official advice on something, find a sentence telling you what
to do, and act on it. The trouble is that the document you opened is not made
of one kind of sentence. Some sentences are the advice itself, the part a
committee argued over with a body of evidence behind it. Others are examples,
suggestions and explanations, written to help you picture the advice or to
round out a booklet. The documents are often open about this, and Australia's
[24-hour movement
guidelines](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians/recommendations-for-adults-18-to-64-years)
print their recommendations and then a separate heading that says "Tips". But
once a sentence is quoted on its own, in a news story or by a friend or by you
a fortnight later, the two kinds look identical. Telling them apart again is
this week's work, and it does not require reading any research. It requires
asking what the document already says about itself, which is reliably the part
nobody reads. Three questions do most of it. Is this a recommendation or a tip?
How sure does the document say it is? Who is it for, and when did anyone last
check it? Each takes seconds, and together they tell you how much weight a
sentence will hold before you rebuild a morning around it.

## Three questions to ask of any sentence

### Is it a recommendation, or a tip?

Not one of the Australian Dietary Guidelines mentions breakfast. There are
[five of
them](https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/guidelines/australian-dietary-guidelines-1-5),
each described as "equally important in terms of public health outcomes", and
they cover energy balance and activity, variety across five food groups,
limiting saturated fat and added salt and added sugars and alcohol,
breastfeeding, and food safety. "Enjoy a healthy breakfast" is a practical tip
in the [summary
booklet](https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/sites/default/files/files/the_guidelines/n55a_australian_dietary_guidelines_summary_book.pdf),
a different document by the same authors, so citing it as a dietary guideline
quietly promotes a tip into a recommendation. The movement guidelines keep the
two kinds apart on the page, and a sentence lifted out of them still loses the
difference. The [adult
recommendations](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians/recommendations-for-adults-18-to-64-years)
are thirty minutes or more of moderate to vigorous activity on most days,
muscle strengthening on two days or more, functional work for mobility and
balance on three, several hours of light activity daily, limiting and breaking
up sedentary time, and seven to nine hours of sleep with consistent bed and
wake times. The seven thousand step figure is nowhere among them; it sits in a
companion statement. Both numbers are worth knowing and neither is a
recommendation, and the difference is not pedantry, because it tells you how
much argument is standing behind the sentence.

  ignorance. It is what happens when a document reaches you through posters,
  booklets and news coverage, each of which keeps the sentences that are
  easiest to picture. You have been reading the summaries, and the summaries
  are not the guidelines.

### How sure does the document say it is?

A modern guideline rates itself twice, and the two ratings are independent.
NHMRC uses [GRADE](https://www.gradeworkinggroup.org/), which it describes as
an approach "to rate the certainty of evidence and the strength of
recommendations". Certainty comes in four levels: high, moderate, low and very
low. Strength comes in two, strong and conditional, and those definitions repay
attention. A strong recommendation means the panel expects almost all fully
informed people to choose it. A conditional recommendation means the panel
expects fully informed people to differ. Read that again, because it is this
course's premise written by the people who write the guidelines: a conditional
recommendation is a formal statement, inside the guideline system itself, that
this is a place where you are meant to decide for yourself. The two ratings
also come apart more often than the words suggest. A review of eight Irish
national clinical guidelines found that 151 of 202 recommendations were strong,
and that only 55 of those 151 rested on high or moderate certainty evidence.

<CertaintyOfStrong />

Two cautions before you carry that anywhere. Those are clinical guidelines,
which are the most carefully graded kind of guidance there is, so the figure
says nothing directly about what you eat or how far you walk; if anything it
flatters the field, because these documents at least published both ratings.
The authors also note that no guideline in the set said openly that it was
making a strong recommendation on weak evidence. The Australian position for a
general reader is different and in one respect worse. The adult movement
guidelines page carries no certainty rating and no strength rating at all, not
one mention of either, with the supporting evidence held in a separate final
report. So for most public guidance the honest answer to how sure the document
is, is that the page will not tell you and you have to go and find out.

### Who is it for, and when was it last looked at?

Week 1 covered who a guideline is written for, and two cheap checks sit
alongside it. The first is scope. The fourth Australian Dietary Guideline is
"Encourage, support and promote breastfeeding", which is addressed to a reader
who may not be feeding anyone, and it is a useful reminder that a guideline
speaks to a population rather than to you. The second is age, in the other
sense. NHMRC states that its [guidelines](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/guidelines)
"have a limited life. They are regularly reviewed and will be updated or
withdrawn in light of important new evidence." The movement guidelines page
prints a date it was last updated, currently 3 June 2026. A recommendation with
no review date is not wrong for that reason, but you cannot tell whether anyone
has looked at it since it was written, and that is a different kind of
uncertainty from the one GRADE measures.

All three questions are the same move: find out what a sentence actually claims
before you act on it. That is this week's lab as well, which asks you to
[operationalise](/glossary/#operationalise) tiredness, to say what you will
count as tired before you try to measure it. A guideline that has not told you
whether a sentence is a recommendation or a tip, and a measure that has not
told you what counts as tired, fail in the same way. Both let you act with
confidence on a sentence whose meaning was never fixed.
