## Course premise

Public guidance is the best summary we have of what helps people in general.
Sleep seven to nine hours ([Hirshkowitz et al.,
2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2014.12.010)). Aim for seven thousand
steps or more (a companion statement to the [24-Hour Movement
Guidelines](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians/recommendations-for-adults-18-to-64-years)).
Enjoy a healthy breakfast (a practical tip in the [Australian Dietary
Guidelines](https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/sites/default/files/files/the_guidelines/n55a_australian_dietary_guidelines_summary_book.pdf)
summary booklet, not one of the five guidelines themselves).
Notice that these are ranges and aims rather than instructions, and that none of
them knows anything about you. This course teaches you to close that gap in
three moves. Realisation: read the guidance accurately, then look at your own
day and find where you actually sit in it. Change: alter one thing, using the
guidance for what to change and how, which later lectures cover in detail.
Rethinking: read what comes back from your body, your mood and what you
actually did, and revise the change against it. Then go round again, because you
will not be the same person in May that you were in March. The unit is a single
day: twenty-four hours observed, adjusted, and observed again. What the course
will not do is hand you a finished answer, because a finished answer belongs to
one person across one stretch of days, and it is no substitute for a doctor.
What it gives you instead is the loop itself, and the judgement to know when a
result is worth acting on.

## Find the guidelines that suit you

Guidance is not one rule for everyone. It has already been narrowed before it
reaches you, and it is worth knowing on what. Three reasons the numbers are
more specific than they look.

### Where you are

The same rule gives different instructions at different latitudes. Australia's
sun rule is a single number: protect yourself whenever the UV index reaches 3,
and below 3 do not, so your skin can make vitamin D. One threshold, over a
country spanning more than thirty degrees of latitude, which means the rule is
on all twelve months of the year in Darwin, ten in Sydney and eight in Hobart.
In the United Kingdom the shortfall runs the other way, and everyone over four
is advised to consider ten micrograms of vitamin D daily from October to March,
because there is not enough sun in those months to make it. Same sentence, and
the instruction inverts: the first thing to find is not the advice but the
advice written for your latitude.

  hemisphere passes about three per cent closer to the sun during its summer,
  and the air is cleaner. Together, roughly fifteen per cent more UV than the
  same latitude in the north. You have just watched the first move of this
  course happen to you.

### When it is

The same rule also changes through the year in one place, which is the table
above read by row instead of by column. Hobart's average noon index is below 3
from May to August and Melbourne's from May to July, the months when sun
protection is not recommended at all, so the same person in the same city is
told to cover up in January and to get some sun in July, for the same reason
both times.
Neither instruction is a revision of the other; the number moved and the rule
did not. A recommendation with no season attached is therefore incomplete, and
the ones worth trusting say when they apply.

### Who you are

Guidance splits on the characteristics that change the answer. The sleep panel
gives seven to nine hours for adults and seven to eight for older adults, so a
figure quoted without an age band is already missing a caveat. The UK vitamin D
advice narrows again inside the country, recommending supplements all year
rather than only in winter for people who are rarely outdoors, who cover most
of their skin, or who have dark skin from Black African, Black Caribbean or
South Asian backgrounds, because melanin absorbs the UV that makes vitamin D.
Read the caveats before the number: they are the part where guidance tells you
whether it is talking to you at all.

<Narrowing />

That last rung is where the narrowing runs out. A category like "dark skin from
South Asian backgrounds" covers enormous variation in skin, in where people
live, in time spent outdoors. It is a better guess than no guess, and it is
still a guess about a group. Guidance narrows from everyone to a group you
belong to, and good guidance says so plainly. The last step of this course is
that same move, carried to the only group with no variation left inside it.

We are in Australia, so these are the three documents to start from. Each one
states who it is written for and what evidence it rests on, which is the part
to read before the numbers.
