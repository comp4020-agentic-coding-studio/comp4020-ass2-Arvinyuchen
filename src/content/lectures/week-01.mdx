## Course premise

Public guidance is the best summary we have of what helps people in general.
Sleep seven to nine hours ([Hirshkowitz et al.,
2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sleh.2014.12.010)). Aim for seven thousand
steps or more (a companion statement to the [24-Hour Movement
Guidelines](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians/recommendations-for-adults-18-to-64-years)).
Enjoy a healthy breakfast (a practical tip in the [Australian Dietary
Guidelines](https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/sites/default/files/files/the_guidelines/n55a_australian_dietary_guidelines_summary_book.pdf)
summary booklet, not one of the five guidelines themselves).
Notice that these are ranges and aims rather than instructions, and that none of
them knows anything about you. This course teaches you to close that gap in
three moves. Realisation: read the guidance accurately, then look at your own
day and find where you actually sit in it. Change: alter one thing, using the
guidance for what to change and how, which later lectures cover in detail.
Rethinking: read what comes back from your body, your mood and what you
actually did, and revise the change against it. Then go round again, because you
will not be the same person in May that you were in March. The unit is a single
day: twenty-four hours observed, adjusted, and observed again. What the course
will not do is hand you a finished answer, because a finished answer belongs to
one person across one stretch of days, and it is no substitute for a doctor.
What it gives you instead is the loop itself, and the judgement to know when a
result is worth acting on.

## Find the guidelines that suit you

Guidance is not one rule for everyone, and the three things it narrows on are
not this course's invention. Explaining how much sun a person needs, ARPANSA
writes that vitamin D from sunlight ["depends on many factors including
location, time of year, day-to-day activities and individual
circumstances"](https://www.arpansa.gov.au/understanding-radiation/radiation-sources/more-radiation-sources/sun-exposure/frequently-asked-questions):
who you are, where you are and when it is, in one sentence from the national
radiation authority. What differs is how openly each one is used. Guidance is
organised by who you are, settles where you are before you read a word of it,
and often leaves when it is for you to work out.

### Who you are

This is the axis guidance is built on, to the point of being its table of
contents. Australia's [24-hour movement
guidelines](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians)
are published as a menu of people under the heading "Find the guidelines that
apply to you": birth to five, five to seventeen, eighteen to sixty-four,
pregnancy, sixty-five and over, and people with disability. The sleep panel
splits the same way, seven to nine hours for adults and seven to eight for
older adults, so a figure quoted without an age band is already missing a
caveat. The UK vitamin D advice narrows again inside the country, recommending
supplements all year rather than only in winter for people who are rarely
outdoors, who cover most of their skin, or who have dark skin from Black
African, Black Caribbean or South Asian backgrounds, because melanin absorbs
the UV that makes vitamin D. Read the caveats before the number: they are the
part where guidance tells you whether it is talking to you at all.

### Where you are

Place is usually settled before you read anything, because a guideline is
written for a country, which is also why the same rule gives different
instructions at different latitudes. Australia's sun rule is a single number:
protect yourself whenever the UV index reaches 3, and below 3 do not, so your
skin can make vitamin D. One threshold, over a country spanning more than
thirty degrees of latitude, which means the rule is on all twelve months of the
year in Darwin, ten in Sydney and eight in Hobart. Only the more careful
guidance divides this up inside the country: Cancer Council notes that northern
areas need protection all year, while in [some southern
areas](https://www.cancer.org.au/cancer-information/causes-and-prevention/sun-safety/vitamin-d)
"there are times of the year when sun protection may not be necessary". In the
United Kingdom the shortfall runs the other way, and everyone over four is
advised to consider ten micrograms of vitamin D daily from October to March,
because there is not enough sun in those months to make it. Same sentence, and
the instruction inverts.

<UvSeason />

Two things the table cannot tell you, and both matter before you lean on any
single number in it. The grid is 1.5 degrees, roughly 165 kilometres, so each
value describes the region around a city rather than the city itself. And
Australia's advice keys off the forecast for the day, while these are monthly
averages under cloud-free skies, so a month averaging just under 3 still
contains days well above it. What the table shows is when the threshold is
typically crossed, not what any particular Tuesday will do.

That is also why the two rows at 35 degrees south are worth reading carefully
rather than triumphantly. Adelaide averages 2.98 in May and Canberra 3.04, so
the rule reads as off in one and on in the other on a difference of six
hundredths of an index point, and the grid places both in the same latitude
band. A gap that small sits well inside what a 1.5 degree monthly average can
actually resolve, so the honest lesson is not about Adelaide or Canberra. It is
that a threshold laid across a smooth gradient will always manufacture pairs
like this, and that is true of every cut-off this course will show you.

<Callout type="info">
  And not for the reason most people think. Cancer Council WA published an
  article titled "Why does Australia have so much skin cancer? (Hint: it's not
  because of an ozone hole)", and the Australian radiation agency's own
  modelling found the contribution of ozone depletion small, and no larger here
  than in the United States despite far greater depletion in the south. The
  duller reasons: Australians live closer to the equator, the southern
  hemisphere passes about three per cent closer to the sun during its summer,
  and the air is cleaner. Together, roughly fifteen per cent more UV than the
  same latitude in the north. You have just watched the first move of this
  course happen to you.
</Callout>

### When it is

Time is the axis guidance most often leaves implicit. Australia's rule is
written as a condition rather than a season, sun protection whenever the index
reaches 3, so the calendar is a consequence you are left to work out. Below is
that work done for two cities. Hobart's average noon index is under 3 from May
to August and Melbourne's from May to July, the months when sun protection is
not recommended at all, so the same person in the same city is told to cover up
in January and to get some sun in July, for the same reason both times. The UK
advice does the opposite and names its months outright, October to March.
Neither instruction is a revision of the other; the number moved and the rule
did not. A recommendation with no season attached is incomplete, and the ones
worth trusting say when they apply.

<UvCurves />

Australia is not the only country keying its sun advice to that number. ARPANSA
recommends sunscreen on days when the UV index is
[forecast to reach 3 or above](https://www.arpansa.gov.au/understanding-radiation/radiation-sources/more-radiation-sources/sun-protection-sunscreen),
the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention says to
[protect your skin](https://www.cdc.gov/skin-cancer/sun-safety/index.html) when
the index is 3 or higher in your area, and Health Canada says
[the same at 3 or higher](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/sun-safety/sun-safety-basics.html).
Read quickly, that is three countries converging on one threshold, which would
make the threshold more trustworthy than any single country's version of it.
Read slowly, it may be one number quoted three times: the [Global Solar UV
Index](https://www.icnirp.org/cms/upload/publications/ICNIRPWHOSolarUVI.pdf),
developed by the World Health Organization with the World Meteorological
Organization, the United Nations Environment Programme and the International
Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection, already states that below an
index of 3 no protective measures are needed and that above the threshold value
of 3 protection is necessary. None of the three national pages says where its
number came from, so all you can honestly conclude is that the agreement has not
been shown to be independent, which is weaker than it first looked and more
useful to know.

The narrowing runs out before it reaches you. A category like "dark skin from
South Asian backgrounds" covers enormous variation in skin, in where people
live, in time spent outdoors. It is a better guess than no guess, and it is
still a guess about a group. Guidance narrows from everyone to a group you
belong to, and good guidance says so plainly. The last step of this course is
that same move, carried to the only group with no variation left inside it.

We are in Australia, so three national documents are the place to start: the
[Australian Dietary Guidelines](https://www.eatforhealth.gov.au/guidelines/guidelines),
the [24-hour movement
guidelines](https://www.health.gov.au/topics/physical-activity/24-hour-movement-guidelines-for-all-australians),
and the [guidelines NHMRC publishes](https://www.nhmrc.gov.au/guidelines) across
everything else it covers. Each states who it is written for and what evidence
it rests on, which is the part to read before the numbers. The map below carries
that closing move outward, and asks what the equivalents are in four other
countries and what shape they arrive in.

<GuidelineMap />

What the colour records is the shape of the document rather than what is inside
it. Canada's guideline for children and youth was, on its own account, the first
evidence-based guidelines to address the whole day, putting activity, sitting and
sleep in one place instead of three. Australia did not repeat that work: its own
development report says the benefit was that it could leverage the considerable
work done in Canada, finishing in far less time and with fewer resources, which
is a health department telling you plainly that it copied, and a good decision.
The United Kingdom and the United States publish activity guidelines of the same
standing in which sleep appears only among the benefits activity brings, never
among the targets that are set.

New Zealand is where two boxes stopped being enough. Its guideline for five to
seventeen year olds is Canada's, adapted with permission and retitled Sit Less,
Move More, Sleep Well, so children there are handed a whole day. Its adults are
handed something nobody else in this set publishes: eating and activity bound
into a single document, the Eating and Activity Guidelines for New Zealand
Adults, whose five activity statements set no sleep target anywhere in them.
That is integration running along a different axis, joining what you eat to how
you move rather than joining the waking hours to the sleeping ones, and it is
how one country manages to be ahead and behind on the same question at once.

Who is allowed to publish matters as much as what gets published, and this is
where the comparison stops being tidy. Australia's guidelines come from a
government department and from NHMRC, a body that develops guidelines for
clinical practice, public health, environmental health and ethics. Canada's
24-hour guideline comes from the Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology, a
professional society rather than a health ministry. The United Kingdom has NICE
sitting roughly where NHMRC sits, and Canada, the United States and New Zealand
had nothing in this reading that fitted that slot cleanly, so their entries
below say so rather than naming a near miss. An honest gap is worth more than a
forced match, because the forced match is the one you would go on to cite.
