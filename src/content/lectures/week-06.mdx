## Where the number came from

The twenty-five minute work block is one person's self-experiment, with a
[sample size](/glossary/#sample-size) of one, no control condition and no
outcome measure beyond whether he felt he had won a bet with himself. That is
not an accusation and it is not a reconstruction: it is the account the author
gives of himself, in the document that put the method in front of everybody.
Francesco Cirillo released [The Pomodoro
Technique](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/joebew42/study-path/master/assets/cirillo-pomodoro-technique.pdf)
as a free paper on 19 October 2006, revised to version 1.3 the following June,
and its preface says the idea came to him "in the late '80s, during my first
years at university", in a slump after his first year exams, looking critically
at how he studied. So he made a bet, "as helpful as it was humiliating": could
he study, really study, for ten minutes? The timekeeper he found was a kitchen
timer shaped like a pomodoro, the Italian for tomato, and the technique is named
after that timer rather than after anything the timer measured. The same paper
dates the method as defined in 1992, taught to individuals from 1998 and to
teams from 1999. None of that is a trial, and Cirillo never says it is. It is a
single subject investigation of exactly the kind this course spends twelve weeks
teaching you to run. What happened to it afterwards is this lecture.

## Three things you find by reading the source

### The paper does not recommend twenty-five minutes

Section 3.2 of Cirillo's paper is headed "The Length of the Pomodoro", and it is
where the technique's own results sit. It concludes that the ideal interval is
"20 to 35 minutes long, 40 minutes at the most", and then says outright that
"the Pomodoro Technique works best with 30-minute time periods". The note
underneath it describes the only sweep of interval lengths anywhere in the
document, and that sweep was not run on Cirillo at all: it was run by work
groups he was mentoring, each allowed to choose its own length provided the
choice rested on observations about effectiveness. They started with hour long
intervals, because twenty-five had seemed too short to them, went up to two
hours, came down to forty-five minutes, dropped to ten, and settled on thirty.
Meanwhile the rules appendix at the back of the same paper still opens "A
Pomodoro Consists of 25 minutes Plus a Five-Minute Break". The document
disagrees with itself in print, and what the world carried away was the number
in the rules rather than the number in the results.

### The one sentence citing evidence cites a study guide

The paper makes a single appeal to evidence outside itself about how long an
interval should be. It states that it has been proven that intervals of
twenty to forty-five minutes "can maximize our attention and mental activity, if
followed by a short break", and it carries a footnote. Reference 15 in the bibliography is "Yona
McGregor e Tony Buzan, A Guide to Animal Farm, Hodder & Stoughton, 1998; ISBN
0-340-66401-0". That ISBN resolves in [Open
Library](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL22578062M/A_guide_to_Animal_farm) to an
eighty-five page volume in the Teach Yourself Literature Guides series, written
by Iona McGregor with Tony Buzan credited as a contributor: a revision guide to
George Orwell's novel. There is no sample size to report because there is no
study to report one from. The point is not that the author was careless with a
footnote. The point is that following it took about a minute, the sentence has
been repeated for two decades, and in two decades that minute was apparently
never spent.

### What reached you is the timer and nothing else

Cirillo now writes about this himself, under the heading
["Distortion"](https://www.pomodorotechnique.com/francesco-cirillo/), and his
complaint is a provenance complaint. Newspapers, bloggers and applications, he
says, reduced the whole method to one instruction, work for twenty-five minutes
and then take a break, and "they didn't even need to read the book". He is
blunter than his readers about what the interval is worth: "The iconic 25-minute
timer sessions aren't the technique." Everything that made it a method, the
recording, the estimating, the handling of interruptions, the explicit
instruction to observe your own results and adjust, was stripped off in
transmission, and the only surviving fragment was a number that its own author
had already revised upward in the results section. This is the general shape of
how advice reaches you, and it is worth naming: transmission keeps whatever is
easiest to repeat, which is almost never the caveat.

<Callout type="info">
  Even the origin has drifted, and the two versions come from the same person.
  The 2006 paper says the bet was "Can you study, really study, for 10 minutes?"
  The account on Cirillo's own site today says the question was "Can I study for
  just two minutes without interruption?" Neither is the invention of a
  journalist, both are first hand, and they are forty years apart. If a detail
  can move that far inside one author's own memory, a detail passed through a
  hundred retellings is not a fact you have. It is a claim you have not checked.
</Callout>

Your [pre-registration](/glossary/#pre-registration) is due this week, and the
case for handing it in before you collect anything is the whole of that history.
At no point in forty years did anyone write down in advance what result would
have counted as twenty-five minutes losing, and without that sentence every
outcome confirms the technique: a good fortnight proves it works, a bad
fortnight means you were not protecting your intervals properly, and a fortnight
where nothing much happened means you need to give it longer. A pre-registration
is the only document in this course that genuinely cannot be written afterwards.
Everything else in it can be reconstructed honestly from memory once you know
how things turned out, but what you would have accepted as failure before you
knew is gone the moment you find out, and no amount of good faith brings it
back.

So the lab runs the trial nobody ran. You compare twenty-five minutes against
one other length, chosen by you, with the order
[counterbalanced](/glossary/#counterbalancing) so that "the second one felt
better" and "I had got the hang of it by then" do not arrive as the same
sentence. Bring a written guess about your own best interval, made before you
start, and notice how much harder it is to be specific than to be confident.
Cirillo's twenty-five minutes is a decent hypothesis, honestly arrived at by one
person with a kitchen timer, and it was never offered as more than that. This
week you find out whether your own answer is any better founded than his was.
